Kenal sambal · what is sambal

Chilli, pounded with whatever the island grows

Sambal is a chilli relish ground in a cobek, a shallow stone mortar, with an ulekan pestle. The chilli is a constant. Everything else changes from island to island: citrus in Sulawesi, smoked fish in Manado, peanuts in East Java, fermented durian in Jambi. Most sambals fall into four families: raw, boiled, cooked in oil and fermented. The same four colours mark their pins on the map.

Chilli is a newcomer

Chillies come from the Americas and reached the archipelago through Portuguese and Spanish trade in the 1500s. Before then, heat came from native long pepper, cabe jawa.

Java has the most

Both major surveys find that Java, the island that grows the most chilli, has the largest share of sambal varieties, followed by Sumatra.

The mortar matters

Pounding crushes cells and releases oils that a blender chops past. A cobek also lets you leave texture: rough for raw sambal, smooth for cooked.

The Spice Islands came first

Long before chilli arrived, cloves from North Maluku and nutmeg from the Banda Islands drew Arab, Chinese, Portuguese and Dutch ships to the archipelago. Rempah built the trade routes that later carried chilli in.

Padanan · what are you eating?

Name a dish, get three sambals

Any cuisine works. Claude picks three sambals from the profiled ones in this atlas and says why each one suits it.

Cut the richness

Coconut curries, rendang and fried food want acid. Raw sambals with lime, tomato or unripe fruit, like dabu-dabu, acan or lado mudo, clean the palate.

Echo the fire

Grilled fish and meat suit smoky or toasted sambals. Roa and cakalang bring smoked fish; terasi brings toasted shrimp.

Fresh with fresh

Seafood and salads go with sliced, uncooked sambals like matah and colo-colo, which add aroma without weight.

Sweet calms the heat

Cooked sambals with palm sugar or peanuts, such as bajak and pecel, are gentler. They suit children, vegetables and anyone new to chilli.

Tentang kami · about us

An atlas of sambal, in a kampung at the foot of Merapi

Indonesia has hundreds of sambals, and most of them never travel further than the next village. Oelek gathers them in one place, in English, so anyone curious can find where each one comes from, what goes into the cobek, what to eat it with, and how to make it at home.

Why Oelek

Ulek, written oelek in the old spelling, is the stone pestle that grinds sambal against the cobek. Most Indonesian kitchens start their sambal with it, and the word travelled abroad on jars of sambal oelek.

The atlas starts from two surveys of Indonesian sambals, including the one by researchers at Universitas Gadjah Mada that counted 257 sambals fitting the definition of a condiment, and adds regional accounts and documented recipes. Every card links to its sources. Profiles describe a representative version, not the one true recipe: every family makes its own.

The atlas now holds 260 sambals in four families, by how the cobek's contents are treated: raw (mentah), pounded or sliced fresh; boiled (rebus), with the chillies boiled or steamed first, like the thin sambal on every mie ayam and bakso cart; cooked (masak), fried or sautéed in oil; and fermented (fermentasi), built on a fermented base such as tempoyak, tauco or rusip.

The kampung behind every page

Behind the atlas is a kampung on the slopes of Merapi, looking north across the sawah to the volcano and Merbabu. It follows your clock: roosters and mist at subuh, egrets over the padi in the morning, cicadas at midday, kites in the afternoon, bats at dusk, then crickets, frogs, the tokek and fireflies, with Merapi glowing red in the dark. After dark the whole page turns to its night colours. The moon shows its real phase, and on some afternoons in the wet season it rains.

The kampung also keeps the year. The sawah goes through three crops: ploughing, tandur, green padi, gold padi, harvest and burning the jerami. After the second harvest there is merti desa, with a kirab of gunungan and wayang kulit all night, and once a year the Kraton's Labuhan procession climbs the slope of Merapi.

Like most villages in Java, its neighbours are a masjid, a gereja, a klenteng and a pura, and each keeps its own days: 17 Agustus with flags, tirakatan and panjat pinang; Ramadan, malam takbiran, Lebaran and Idul Adha; Sekaten and the quiet of malam 1 Suro; Natal and Paskah; the ogoh-ogoh before Nyepi; lanterns rising from Borobudur on Waisak night; Imlek and Cap Go Meh with the barongsai; and fireworks for the new year.

The sounds

The round speaker button at the bottom left plays the kampung. Nothing is a recording: every sound is made in your browser as it plays, so it never repeats exactly. Kutilang and prenjak in the morning, a hen, sapi and kambing in the kandang, a sapu lidi in the yard, a motorbike on the road, the tukang bakso's tok-tok, the sendaren humming on the kites, rain on the genteng, and at night the frogs, the jangkrik, the tokek and the ronda's kentongan striking the hour, with a neighbour's gamelan practising in the evening.

The bedug sounds before each prayer and the gereja bells ring on Sunday mornings and on feast days. We chose not to imitate voices: there is no synthesised adzan, takbir, sinden or choir, because a machine imitation would not do them justice. The bedug, the bells and the gamelan carry those moments instead.

Sources and notes

Names, regional associations and defining ingredients come from the surveys and linked regional accounts. Profiles are source-informed editorial descriptions of representative versions: supporting seasonings may be reconstructed where a survey gives only the defining ingredient. Serving suggestions, heat levels, flavour scores, illustrations and persona texts are editorial, not measurements or claims of a single traditional recipe. Province-only records are not assigned precise map coordinates: they appear as hollow pins grouped at the province centre. Most sambals are now made across Indonesia; the map is a simplified drawing.
